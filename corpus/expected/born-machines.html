The Constraint That Quietly Breaks Your Optimizer

  • A probability table over 10 binary variables lives on a 1,023-dimensional simplex: every coordinate non-negative, all summing to one — two constraints that are never just ‘there for free’.
  • Softmax parameterization dissolves the sum-to-one constraint but smuggles in a normalization constant that couples every parameter to every other, so a gradient step on one logit implicitly shifts the meaning of all others.
  • Clamping and projection approaches create hard boundary walls: gradients point outward but the iterate can’t move, producing zero-gradient stalls that look deceptively like convergence.
  • Even well-behaved models trained with MLE suffer: the log-likelihood gradient near zero-probability outcomes involves dividing by small numbers, creating the numerical instability that practitioners paper over with label smoothing and epsilon floors.
  • The core issue is structural, not incidental — any parameterization of probabilities must encode non-negativity somewhere, and that encoding always costs something in optimization geometry.

Amplitudes: Turning the Constraint Into a Free Lunch

  • Instead of storing a probability p(x) per outcome, store a complex number ψ(x) ∈ ℂ — no sign restriction, no coupling between entries, genuinely unconstrained.
  • Define p(x) = |ψ(x)|² / Σ_x’ |ψ(x’)|²: squaring magnitudes guarantees non-negativity for free, and the single global normalization constant is the only coupling that remains.
  • Every point in ℂ^N parameter space maps to a valid distribution — there is no invalid region, no boundary to approach, no wall for a gradient to bounce off.
  • The global normalization constant Z = Σ |ψ(x)|² is a scalar that touches all parameters symmetrically; it creates coupling but not the asymmetric, outcome-specific pathologies of softmax.
  • This is not an approximation or a relaxation — the mapping from amplitudes to distributions is exact and lossless; every distribution over N outcomes has at least one amplitude preimage.

The Born Rule: Quantum Physics Hands Us the Formula

  • In quantum mechanics, the state of a system is described by a wavefunction ψ: an assignment of a complex amplitude to each possible outcome of a measurement.
  • The Born rule states that the probability of observing outcome x is p(x) = |ψ(x)|², normalized over all outcomes — the exact prescription we just derived from optimization geometry alone.
  • A ‘Born machine’ is therefore any generative model that stores amplitudes and reads off probabilities via the Born rule; the name imports the formula, not the physics or the hardware.
  • No quantum computer is needed: the amplitudes are ordinary complex-valued parameters stored in classical memory and optimized with classical gradient methods — ‘quantum-inspired’ means the mathematical structure, not the substrate.
  • This reframing matters because quantum physicists have spent decades studying what amplitude representations can and cannot express — Born machines let ML researchers inherit those results directly.

Training Still Works: Maximum Likelihood Through Amplitudes

  • The log-likelihood of a dataset under a Born machine is ℓ(ψ) = Σ_{x ∈ D} log(|ψ(x)|²) − |D| · log Z, where Z = Σ_{x’} |ψ(x’)|².
  • Differentiating with respect to a real or complex amplitude ψ(x) yields a clean gradient: a data term pushing ψ(x) to be larger for observed outcomes, and a model-expectation term pulling it back — the same push-pull structure as in classical MLE.
  • Crucially, neither term involves dividing by |ψ(x)|, so gradients are well-defined everywhere in parameter space, including near zero — the epsilon-floor problem is structurally absent.
  • For complex amplitudes, ‘differentiating’ uses Wirtinger calculus, treating ψ and ψ* as independent variables; the result is a straightforward extension that automatic differentiation libraries handle natively.
  • Mini-batch SGD, Adam, and standard regularization all apply without modification — the Born machine is a drop-in replacement for the probability table, not a foreign object requiring a new training philosophy.

Scaling Up: Factorizing the Amplitude Tensor

  • For n binary variables, the full amplitude tensor ψ has 2^n entries — exponential storage that must be compressed before the model is useful beyond toy problems.
  • Tensor-network factorizations — specifically matrix product states (MPS) — represent ψ as a chain of small tensors, one per variable, connected by shared ‘bond’ indices of dimension χ.
  • The bond dimension χ is a single knob: χ = 1 gives a fully factorized (naïve mean-field) amplitude; increasing χ adds entanglement and expressivity in controllable increments.
  • Sampling and computing the normalization constant Z both remain tractable under MPS structure via sequential contraction, keeping inference costs linear in n and polynomial in χ.
  • MPS Born machines are not a new architectural invention — they are exactly the tensor-train format that appeared in numerical analysis and quantum many-body physics, now reused as a generative model; the Born rule is what makes the connection natural.

Interference: Why Amplitudes Buy Expressivity Probabilities Cannot

  • Because amplitudes are signed or complex, they can cancel: two paths contributing ψ = +0.5 and ψ = −0.5 to the same outcome produce net amplitude zero and thus probability zero — destructive interference.
  • A classical factorization of probabilities — writing p(x) as a product of non-negative factors — cannot produce a zero probability for an outcome unless at least one factor is exactly zero; cancellation between positive terms is structurally forbidden.
  • This asymmetry lets a Born machine represent distribution families that require exponentially larger classical models to approximate: the correlation structure encoded by interference is genuinely richer than the correlation structure encodable by positive factorizations of the same size.
  • Concretely: there exist distributions over n bits where an MPS Born machine of bond dimension χ achieves exact representation, while any matrix product operator on probabilities needs bond dimension exponential in n to match it — a proven separation, not a conjecture.
  • Interference is not a quirk of the physics metaphor; it is a mathematical property of signed real or complex factorizations vs. non-negative factorizations, and it is the precise reason the amplitude substitution widens the model class.

What ‘Quantum-Inspired Expressivity Guarantees’ Actually Means

  • The expressivity guarantees are not about quantum speedup or quantum hardware — they are theorems about the function classes representable by amplitude-based vs. probability-based tensor factorizations of the same size.
  • Classical models optimizing over the probability simplex are constrained to the cone of non-negative tensors; Born machines optimize over all of ℂ^N and project to probabilities only at readout — a strictly larger search space.
  • The ‘quantum-inspired’ qualifier is precise: the results were proved in the context of quantum many-body physics and quantum circuit expressivity, and Born machines import them by structural analogy, not by running on quantum hardware.
  • What classical generative models lack is not a missing architectural trick but a fundamental geometric limitation — any model that stores probabilities directly or as products of non-negative factors hits the same non-negative-factorization ceiling.
  • The single substitution — amplitude instead of probability — is therefore not a numerical convenience or a reparameterization trick; it moves the model into a different expressivity class with provably representable distributions that the original class cannot reach at equal parameter count.

Limitations, Open Questions, and Where Born Machines Fit

  • The normalization constant Z is a sum over all 2^n outcomes; for MPS it is tractable, but for more expressive tensor networks (e.g., PEPS, deep circuits) it becomes #P-hard — the tractability advantage is architecture-dependent.
  • Complex phases in ψ contribute to interference but are unobservable in p(x) = |ψ|² alone; they are only meaningful relative to other amplitudes, which means real-valued amplitudes (signed, not complex) often suffice in practice and simplify implementation.
  • Born machines are generative models for discrete distributions; extending to continuous domains requires different amplitude parameterizations (e.g., quantum field theory analogues) that are less mature.
  • Training stability and hyperparameter sensitivity in MPS Born machines are not yet as well-characterized as in VAEs or normalizing flows — practitioners inherit the expressivity gains but also the relative immaturity of the tooling.
  • The open question is which real-world datasets live in the part of distribution space where interference expressivity matters; theoretical separations exist on constructed examples, and empirical evidence on benchmark datasets is still accumulating.
